Assumptions are not always the taboo word they have made to be. Every single advancement of the human race first started with a logical conclusion or expirement following an assumption on something being true. However, we are all aware when assumptions can, and do fail. These can be equally as helpful for our advancement as well. Whether it is using it to demonstrate why something definetly *can't* be true, to furthering our understanding on a theory, or (a stats favorite) reject the Null Hypothesis.

One scenario (out of countless) where our assumptions have failed us, is with the Gaussian/Normal distribution underlying those jagged, random movements of the stock market, and people have lost their ability to buy a third yacht because of it. This has led many quants to either choose distributions that tend to exihbit fat tails to match the markets better, or simply throw out any assumptions they have about underlying distrubtions, and let the data itself do the talking. My stab at generating signals from the market is fully based on the latter, topped up with a proprietary, non-parametric test of my own.

I want to make one thing clear. The test we built isn't peer-reviwed, isn't published in any journal, and isn't a verified thesis. What I am claiming however, is that certain parametric assumptions failing to represent financial data is a documented issue, and the approach I've taken is my own attempt at addressing it; grounded in the standard toolkit of statistics, but built and tested independently. I haven't won any national math competions in China, my name isn't Jiang, and I can't say for certain that BB and BBB bonds go bust at 8% default.

### The Test
The math behind this project starts where many practitioners of finance do: getting volatility, returns, and momentum. A big focus is on rolling statistics, each optimized on their windows to account for stability and reactiveness. When it comes to volatility, we *do not* focus on robust variance estimators, but rather estimators which capture the *total* market variance, and do so effectively in a short time frame. While this is rather counter-intuitive to the majority of the literature, the setup of our particular test will hopefully shine some light into the issue. The test itself calculates a rolling score, and a rolling threshold, each being dynamic. The previous metrics of returns, volatility, and volume are essentially normalized by *only* looking at the quantiles and the corresponding order statistics within their local window. Once they are, the test combines these to calculate the score, which measures "the total market energy" at that time stamp. This is directly compared to the dynamic threshold as well. If the score passes the threshold, then we state that the current time-step exhibits characteristics of "extreme" moves.

By this approach, this test is robust against temporal changes of the underlying data (an issue that is well known for something like the LM test), is regime aware and dynamic, and most importantly, we are able to separate typical market moves from extreme ones through pure non-parametric means. However, this is not a silver bullet. The test's accuracy and precision are very sensitive to the local windows used to compare the current observation to its neighbours, and to a hyperparameter, which takes a role not too dissimilar to something like the *alpha* in normal hypothesis testing and statistical tests, but does not technically fit the formal definition.

Furthermore, while in our case we (thankfully) have the power of modern hardware to run it so its less of an issue, this test doesn't converge to a set distribution that we can use the results of and speed up the process. Sometimes we are lucky in that regard coming from a non-parametric approach, while other times, we are stuck with what we have. This also means it makes it hard to study it analytically, and we have many less properties to depend on when we do try and study it.

So, why did we choose this approach in the first place? Simple. We were willing to let go of these nice academic properties to capture the real underlying distribution of the market, warts and all.

### A Quick (And Slightly Technical) History Of Why
Many practitioners of quantitative finance, and statistics as a whole, have heard about Brownian Motion. They also know the issue with assuming stocks follow that stochastic process. It works great, until it doesn't. The Gaussian/normal assumption underlying that process simply fails to capture the full picture. Extreme movements are more common in these markets than a bell-curve would suggest.

To mitigate this, Merton (one of the authors of the original Black-Scholes formula, which yes, he is not usually named when we talk about the formula, but it *is* technically called Black-Scholes-Merton, but I digress) decided to add something else. He takes Brownian Motion, and adds a term to it. This term not only counts the extreme events as they occur randomly, but also allows the magnitude of that extreme event to vary. Mathematically, we are saying "hey, these black-swan type events happen, and also they are not always the same. Sometimes we can see a drop of 20%, and sometimes we see a drop of 30%." Perfect, now we are getting somewhere. Mathematically, this would be a Compound Poisson Process.

The only issue is, Merton assumed those shocks themselves also follow a log-normal distribution (Gaussian once you take the log). But, it was much better at describing the data, and the base idea was sound.

Soon, other academics and practitioners came up with their own models. All assuming Brownian Motion, plus a term that counts extreme events. What changes is the distribution they use to model those extreme events itself. For example, the Kou Model assumes that the extreme movements follow a double exponential distribution. This not only captures those so-called "fat tails," and probability of extreme events better, but it also lets an extreme upswing and a market crash both have a say in how it weighs and shapes the distribution, instead of just clumping it all together. The model also allows itself to be easier to interpret and connect back to real instruments or events in the market.

These more complex models end up explaining more of the market, yet we start to notice something strange. Even Merton's attempt at rectifying pure Brownian Motion only has a semi-closed-form solution, an infinite sum of Black-Scholes terms. In fact, for many of these models (like Kou), we can only solve them numerically. If this is the case, and we end up with approximations and not clean results anyways, then why not go all the way?

While these are all useful and incredibly valuable works, what if we abondon analyatical solutions and take a different approach? We can agree that the market has typical movements, and extreme ones that we can count, each with a different magnitude. But, we also reject the underlying distributions as a whole. We will accept that it is a stochastic process, but we deny any pure, beautiful mathematical shape, and instead only accept what we see in the data itself. This right here, is the absolute core of Project Bellwether. We only allow ourselves to let the data itself tell us what is typical and what is extreme.

### So... are you rich now?
Yeah dude, my Koenigsegg Gemera ships this week. 

Now, while I am being a bit facetious here, a successful trading strategy is arguably more dependent on risk management than pure signal processing. While this project and approach helps with the signal portion, it greatly helps assess risk. Almost by definition, we have accounted for the true tail-risk of the market. 

However, while this article is spent on why a non-parametric approach is better, like everything in statistics, it comes with its weaknesses. The biggest issue with a non-parametric approach, especially where past performance is not indicative of future performance, is overfitting. It is surprisingly easy to overfit our data with this approach, and great care is taken to ensure we reduce that risk as much as possible. 

Another issue is a relative lack of "power" when compared to parametric methods. For the same number of observations, parametric methods almost always give more accurate statistics than their non-parametric counterpart. If we understand the downsides of this approach, and take that into account, then this process has real promise. In fact, preliminary tests on tree-ensembles trained through this approach have given us a suite of models well versed in assessing dollars at risk, and categorization. Certain models have also shown promise for short-term forecasting, however, further testing is needed before those models can be put into production. 

As this project grows, we hope to see the continued success of this approach, while hoping to test and push limitations that might not have arisen yet. However for now, this is a great start. No swimming in a pool of money like Scrooge McDuck in the near future, but that dream keeps me going, and the models are hinting that it is possible.
